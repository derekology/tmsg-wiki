title: Account-Based Marketing
description: A practical guide to account-based marketing, from account selection to coordinated engagement.

Account-based marketing (ABM) is a B2B approach that plans marketing around selected organizations rather than treating every individual lead as an independent target. Marketing, sales, and sometimes customer success coordinate around account priorities, relevant buying groups, and agreed outcomes. ABM is not a channel or a single software category; it is an operating strategy that can use advertising, sales outreach, events, content, and customer programs.

## How an ABM program works

1. **Agree on the ideal account profile.** Define fit using evidence such as business model, needs, potential value, and ability to serve the customer. See [ideal customer profile](/concepts/ideal-customer-profile/).
2. **Select and tier accounts.** Prioritize a manageable set based on strategic value and realistic capacity. A small number may receive individually tailored work; larger groups may receive segment-level programs.
3. **Map people and needs.** Identify relevant roles in the buying group rather than assuming one contact represents the whole organization.
4. **Coordinate engagement.** Align useful content, advertising, sales activity, and follow-up to the account's situation.
5. **Review progress together.** Use shared definitions for account engagement, opportunity progression, and revenue contribution.

## How it differs from lead generation

Lead programs often begin with an individual action, such as completing a form, and then qualify that person. ABM starts with an account or buying group and asks which people and interactions matter within it. The approaches can work together: account strategy can guide which leads to prioritize, while individual engagement can provide signals about an account.

## When it is useful—and when it is not

ABM may be appropriate when a business sells to a defined set of organizations, several stakeholders influence a purchase, and teams can coordinate tailored engagement. It is not automatically superior to broad demand programs. If account selection is weak, data is unreliable, or sales and marketing cannot agree on ownership, the program may simply relabel existing campaigns.

Compare ABM outcomes with the cost and time invested; do not rely on activity counts alone. Vendor benchmarks can be useful context, but figures from a platform's customer base are not a universal forecast.
