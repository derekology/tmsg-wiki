The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) is the European Union regulation governing the processing of personal data. It applies to organizations in the circumstances set out in the Regulation, including certain organizations outside the EU that offer goods or services to, or monitor the behavior of, people in the EU. The [official text](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/2016-05-04/eng) defines the scope, principles, lawful bases, individual rights, and controller and processor responsibilities.

## Implications for marketing

Marketing teams should be able to explain what personal data they use, why they use it, how long they retain it, who receives it, and how people can exercise their rights. The GDPR requires a lawful basis for each processing purpose and principles including transparency, purpose limitation, data minimisation, accuracy, storage limitation, security, and accountability. A lawful basis for processing personal data is not, by itself, permission to send marketing through every channel.

For direct marketing, Article 21 gives people the right to object at any time to processing for that purpose, including related profiling; after an objection, the data may no longer be processed for direct marketing. The European Commission also explains that electronic marketing is subject to additional national rules implementing the ePrivacy Directive. Therefore, email and SMS permission requirements can depend on the channel and country, and should not be reduced to a single universal rule.

Consent, where used as the lawful basis, must meet the GDPR's conditions and be withdrawable. Keep evidence of the preference and propagate it to relevant systems. A suppression record may need to be retained to prevent future marketing; deletion requests should be assessed against applicable legal obligations and the specific facts rather than automatically erasing every record.
