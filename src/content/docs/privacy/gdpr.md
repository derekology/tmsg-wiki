The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) is the European Union's general framework for protecting personal data. It applies to processing by organizations established in the EU and, in defined circumstances, to organizations outside the EU that offer goods or services to people in the EU or monitor their behavior there. The Regulation addresses data-protection principles, legal bases, individual rights, and responsibilities of controllers and processors.

**Not legal advice:** This page is a general educational overview, not legal advice or a compliance assessment. The GDPR's application depends on the facts, local law, and related rules. Consult qualified legal counsel familiar with the relevant jurisdiction and activity for advice.

## What it covers

Personal data is information relating to an identified or identifiable person. The GDPR requires processing to follow principles such as lawfulness, fairness and transparency; purpose limitation; data minimisation; accuracy; storage limitation; security; and accountability. It recognizes several possible legal bases for processing, including consent, contract, legal obligations, and legitimate interests; which basis can apply depends on the processing and circumstances.

The Regulation gives people rights that include access, rectification, erasure, restriction, portability, and objection, subject to the conditions and exceptions in the law. It distinguishes controllers, which determine purposes and means, from processors that handle data for a controller, and sets responsibilities for both. Other topics include security, personal-data-breach notifications, impact assessments in specified cases, and transfers of personal data outside the European Economic Area.

For example, using customer records to segment an advertising audience can involve processing personal data. Whether a particular campaign or channel is permitted is a separate, fact-specific question; electronic marketing can also be subject to the ePrivacy Directive and national rules.
